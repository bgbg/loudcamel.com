The narrative CV that stands out: what funders actually read

By Boris Gorelik · August 21, 2026

You open the funding call expecting the usual “list your publications,” and instead you get four empty text boxes. How did you contribute to knowledge? To individuals? To your community? To society? A thousand words, split however you like. A list of papers you could write in your sleep, and next to it, a story about your own contribution that you’ve never once written down.

That is a narrative CV, and funders are switching to it fast. The official templates from UKRI, the Royal Society, and your university’s research office tell you what each of the four boxes is for. Almost none of them tell you how to write something a reviewer will actually remember. This post is that second half: replace vague claims with specific evidence, one contribution at a time.

Which funders actually want a narrative CV?

The format began at the Royal Society in 2019 as the “Résumé for Researchers.” UKRI adopted its own version, the Résumé for Research and Innovation, or R4RI. In 2021 a Joint Funders Group signed a shared statement committing to the approach, and the signatories include UKRI, Wellcome, Cancer Research UK, the British Heart Foundation, Alzheimer’s Research UK, the NIHR, and the Royal Academy of Engineering. Several European funders are piloting their own.

The wording differs a little between them, but they share four modules:

  1. your contribution to the generation of knowledge,
  2. your contribution to the development of individuals and teams,
  3. your contribution to the wider research community,
  4. your contribution to broader society.

UKRI’s version adds an optional box for context: career breaks, secondments, and the like. Most calls give you around 1,000 words in total, split however you want, written in the first person.

Why is this harder than a publication list?

Because a publication list hides behind numbers. Forty papers, an h-index, three grants: the reader supplies the meaning. A narrative CV takes that crutch away. You’ve to say what you did and why it mattered, in plain words, and a reviewer can tell in one sentence whether you’re showing evidence or reciting a job description.

The usual failure is vagueness. “I have made significant contributions to my field and mentored many students.” That sentence could belong to anyone. It feels safe, which is exactly why people write it. It also says nothing, and a reviewer working through forty applications won’t remember yours.

How do you make each of the four modules land?

One rule covers all four: delete the adjective and write the evidence instead. Every time you reach for “influential” or “significant,” cut it and describe the specific thing that earned the word.

Knowledge. Don’t write “I published widely in leading journals.” Write what changed because of your work. “I developed a calibration method that three other labs now use to measure X” tells a reviewer more than any impact factor. Name the idea, and name who picked it up.

Development of individuals. Don’t write “I have supervised many students.” Write “I supervised a PhD student who arrived with no coding background and who now leads data engineering at [type of organisation].” One person described in real detail beats a headcount. Show the before and the after.

Wider community. Don’t write “I am active in my research community.” Write the specific act and its size. “I review for four journals, and I rebuilt our department seminar from two talks a term into a fortnightly series that now brings in external speakers.” Verbs and numbers.

Broader society. This is the box people pad the most. Don’t write “my research has real-world impact.” Write who outside academia used it, and how. “I wrote the plain-language guidance a patient charity now sends to newly diagnosed families.” If nobody outside academia has used your work yet, say plainly what you did to try. Reviewers can smell an inflated impact claim from across the room.

Notice the shape of every “do” example: a specific action, a named beneficiary or a real number, and a consequence. That is the whole trick, repeated four times.

What if your evidence is genuinely thin?

Then be honest, and be small and specific. One real, concrete example of mentoring a single person well reads as more credible than a paragraph of confident abstraction. You control how the word count is split, so put the words where your evidence is strongest and keep the thin module short. Nobody is strong in all four, and reviewers know that.

And write in the first person, in active language: led, built, taught, changed. UKRI’s own guidance asks for exactly this. Flat committee-speak is the second most common way these CVs go dead, right behind vagueness.

I’ve not sat on a UKRI panel, and reviewers differ. But every piece of funder guidance I read while writing this points the same way. Specific and honest beats grand and vague.

Common questions

Which funders require a narrative CV? UKRI (as the R4RI), the Royal Society, Wellcome, Cancer Research UK, the British Heart Foundation, Alzheimer’s Research UK, the NIHR, and the Royal Academy of Engineering have all committed to the narrative CV approach, with more funders piloting it. Always check the specific call, because wording and word limits vary.

How long should a narrative CV be? Most funders allow around 1,000 words across the four modules, written in the first person, and let you decide how to split the total.

What is the single most common mistake? Vagueness. Claims like “significant contributions” and “widely published” say nothing on their own. Replace every such phrase with a specific action, a named beneficiary or a number, and what changed.
